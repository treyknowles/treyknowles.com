Developers don't want to be sold to. They want to discover something useful, try it in five minutes, and tell their team about it on their own terms. That's the core tension in devtools go-to-market — the best path to revenue is often to stay out of the way.

## The product *is* the pitch

In a traditional B2B sale, the product demo is theater. You control the script, you pick the happy path, and you answer objections in real time. Developers see through this immediately. The moment a developer senses a canned demo, they've already moved on.

The best devtools companies make the product the pitch. Vercel's first impression is `npx create-next-app` followed by `vercel deploy`. If that experience doesn't sell you, no amount of sales calls will. The job of GTM at Vercel wasn't to convince developers — it was to make sure the right developers found that experience.

## Distribution is community, not pipeline

Traditional SaaS GTM is about building pipeline: MQLs, outbound sequences, SDR/AE splits. Devtools GTM is about building community: Discord servers, GitHub discussions, conference talks, open-source contributions.

The value of a developer advocate who writes a great tutorial isn't captured in Salesforce. But it might bring in 50 enterprise deals two years later when those developers become CTOs.

## Bottoms-up, then land-and-expand

The pattern that actually works: individual developers adopt because it solves a real problem. Usage spreads organically across a team. At some inflection point, a procurement conversation starts — and by then, you're not selling in, you're just formalizing what already exists.

The GTM implication: obsess over the individual developer experience first. The enterprise contract is a lagging indicator of developer love.
